# Evidence: every number and rule, with its source and its limits

This file lists each value the app shows or uses. Nothing here is a clinical accuracy claim. The tool is a hackathon prototype for assessment and next steps, not diagnosis.

Evidence grades: **A** physics or a published standard; **B** real data from a small group; **C** common statistical convention or expert practice; **D** carried over from Earth practice, not tested in spaceflight; **None** a build placeholder with no evidence behind it.

## What the real data does and does not show

On the "Real crew data" screen, one crew member's haemoglobin sat below the laboratory range in one of their own pre-flight draws. A population range alone would flag a value that is usual for that person, which is the reason for comparing each reading with a personal baseline. This is one observation in four people. It illustrates the idea; it does not prove it.

## Known limits

- Every rule number and every line of advice is a placeholder until a clinician on the team signs it off. The app shows this on screen and shows no review badge.
- The band factor of 2, the trend window of 7 readings, the shift of 1 standard deviation, the sustained count of 3 and the combined minimum of 2 flagged signals are team choices based on statistical convention. They have not been tested against real in-flight data or real cases.
- A 14-day baseline, and a 3-draw haemoglobin baseline, are small samples. Normal variation in haemoglobin is estimated from only four crew members' pre-flight draws.
- The cabin carbon dioxide limit is a habitat design requirement. It is not a clinical alert threshold.
- No space-specific alert bands were found in published sources for haemoglobin, heart rate, sleep, mood or reaction time Blood pressure, visual acuity, exercise plan done, body mass, fluid intake and food energy are judged only against each person's own range. Any such band is a team decision.
- The fifteen indicators cover the main spaceflight health areas and await clinician confirmation (CR-01). The team's challenge brief advises choosing five to seven indicators and doing them well. The team lead chose breadth (D-015). Complete blood counts are not a routine in-flight self-measurement today.
- The grip strength floor and the career radiation limit (NASA-STD-3001 Volume 1) are programme requirements, not clinical alert thresholds. Using them as prompts is the team's choice. The radiation share shown counts this mission only, not earlier flights.
- The visual acuity self-check is uncalibrated. It is not a calibrated eye chart and not a test for any eye condition. Screen, lighting and distance change its result.
- There is no in-flight way to measure bone directly. Exercise and grip strength stand in for it.
- Every value from the clinical lead's build pack v3.3 (the monitor alert thresholds, the display bands, the lapse time, the adapting period, the crew check-in items and the urine strip note) is a placeholder until signed off.
- Built on 15 Nov 2026 after the mentors' feedback: voice on Vital signs and the daily check-in, and device reading (a simulated kit, SYNTHETIC, and readers for the published Bluetooth health formats, checked by automated tests against example data).
- Not built: speech recognition that runs on the device with no internet, device links tested with flight hardware, a rover profile, rules that change by environment, any calibrated or clinical eye test (for example retinal imaging), and any direct bone measurement.
- Synthetic results are never evidence of clinical accuracy.

## Where this sits beside existing work

NASA and the Canadian Space Agency already work on medical decision support and on wearable monitoring for crews, and on care that depends less on Earth. This prototype adds no sensors and replaces no flight surgeon or crew medical officer. It is a hackathon-scale sketch of one crew-facing slice: a routine daily check-in, rules that show their source, a view of slow trends, and a clear picture of how long a reply will take and what to do meanwhile. It is not connected to any NASA system, and NASA does not endorse it.
