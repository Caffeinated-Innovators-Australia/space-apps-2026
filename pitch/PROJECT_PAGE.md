## High-level summary

On the way to Mars, a question to Earth's doctors can take up to 45 minutes to come back, and astronauts are not assumed to be medical experts. Our tool helps them act in that gap. Take Jo, on Day 214 at a Mars base, 18 light-minutes from Earth. For three days her fluid intake has been low, so the tool raises a Watch early warning that names kidney stone risk, with NASA's evidence report as its source. Then pain starts. Three taps later the pattern is Act now: the rules say who to tell and what to do first, including a urine test strip and a guided ultrasound, and a case goes to Earth as a clinical handover. A countdown shows that no answer can arrive for 36 minutes, so the crew starts the steps. The doctor's reply lands as a step. Later a fever with low blood pressure or fast breathing raises a possible-sepsis alert that opens emergency mode. Then the same case runs for Sam, a station hand at a Simpson Desert station, 640 km from the nearest hospital. The gap is the same: in very remote Australia, potentially avoidable deaths run at 252 per 100,000 people against 88 in major cities. Behind that one story the tool watches fifteen indicators against each person's own baseline, fits its check-ins around the crew's day, and rewards honest reporting, never good numbers. Rules decide; no AI model runs in the app.

## Detailed project description

**What it does.** The demo is one story: Day 214 on a Mars base. The astronaut checks in on the indicators due that day. Most are compared with their own baseline, a band of their own mean plus or minus 2 standard deviations. Jo's sleep has slid over three weeks while every night stayed inside the band, so a trend rule flags it. Cabin carbon dioxide reads 3.4 mmHg, above NASA's cabin limit of 3 mmHg. Either signal prompts a reaction-time check. Every indicator has an explanation card: how to measure, why it matters, what to do, the NASA hazards it relates to, and sources. When two or more signals are flagged on the same day, they are shown together on one card. Each result is a tier, shown as colour, shape and word: Nothing to do (green circle), Watch (amber triangle), Act now (red square). One tap sends a case pack to Earth and opens a countdown to the earliest possible reply, with the steps to take meanwhile.

**Fifteen indicators in seven groups.** Mind and sleep: sleep, mood, reaction time (a PVT-style tap test). Heart: resting heart rate, blood pressure upper and lower. Eyes: near vision, a self-check in the app, one eye at a time; it is not a calibrated eye chart and not a test for any eye condition, and the app says so. Muscle and bone: exercise plan done, grip strength, body mass; with no in-flight way to measure bone, these stand in for it. Food and fluid: fluid intake, food energy. Blood: haemoglobin. Cabin and radiation: cabin carbon dioxide, and radiation dose as a running total against NASA's career limit; both SYNTHETIC in the demo.

**Built around the crew's day.** A prompt scheduler runs on a mock crew timeline. It never prompts during a critical operation (the demo EVA, 08:00 to 12:00), holds those check-ins until five minutes after it ends, and re-plans when the EVA runs 45 minutes late. An honest-reporting loop keeps the data true: a streak counts check-ins, never results; a shared crew goal has no ranking; "Nothing to report" is a valid one-tap log; insights unlock at 14 and 30 days of streak. A "Who sees what" table on the "Privacy and limits" screen shows what the crew medic, the flight surgeon and the rest of the crew can see (a draft policy for the team to confirm).

**When symptoms form a pattern.** A medical event is more than one number. If an astronaut logs pain in the side or lower back, the app asks three quick questions. If the answers match the pattern a crew medic checks for a kidney stone, a risk NASA names for spaceflight, it raises an Act now result, gives the steps to start now (written by our clinical lead), including a urine test strip and a guided ultrasound, keeps a pain log and builds a case for Earth with the person's recent fluid and exercise trends. The ultrasound says only what to look for; it keeps no image and never says what an image means. It names a pattern to check, never a diagnosis. The symptoms, tiers and steps are placeholders for our clinicians to write.

**Built to be read at a glance.** Our clinicians reviewed the screens and told us they carried too many words for a crew member at work. We rebuilt them. Today now opens on one mark, a shape, a colour and a count, with one tile for each thing that needs attention and a body figure to tap. What is fine is not shown. Reasons and sources are one tap away. The first screen went from 991 words over five screens to under 150 on one.

**Both ends of the conversation.** A sent case is a handover in the order clinicians use: identify, situation, background, assessment, request. The remote doctor reads it in an Earth inbox and replies, and the reply lands back with the crew as steps they can add. If there is no link, the case queues. An early warning comes before any symptom: three days of low fluid raise a Watch result that names kidney stone risk, with NASA's evidence report as the source. An Emergency button on every screen opens basic life support steps one at a time, read aloud. Its wording is a placeholder taken from a published guideline.

**When it becomes an emergency.** Vital signs (temperature, pulse, blood pressure, breathing rate and oxygen saturation) run through two monitor alert rules in the same rules file: possible sepsis and possible shock. Oxygen saturation never raises an alert on its own. An alert is Act now, shows the readings that raised it, and opens emergency mode: the team's emergency module, a separate team prototype shown inside the app and fed our readings. Its checklists are adapted from the NASA Medical Checklist (JSC-48031). It is not for clinical use: some checklists hold medicine names and doses that still need clinical review, and these never appear on our own screens. Its percentage match ranks which checklist to open; it is not a diagnosis. Every threshold is a placeholder from our clinical lead, awaiting sign-off.

**Five environments.** A switcher shows the same case at a Mars base, in transit, on a Moon base, on a space station and in very remote Australia. Each changes the reply delay, who answers, the dominant risks and the guidance line, and carries its gravity, cabin atmosphere and what changes there. The rules themselves do not yet change by environment; that is a clinician decision.

**How it answers the challenge.** It gathers health indicators (typed, tapped or measured readings), lets the astronaut evaluate them (personal baseline, trend, tier, explanation) and act (steps for every tier, escalation with a case pack). It takes up the challenge's considerations: a clickable body figure, different environments, prompting measurements in the mission routine, gamification, simple instructions and more than one kind of input. Each ask in the challenge statement is mapped to a screen in docs/CHALLENGE.md in our repository.

**How it works.** A static web app that runs entirely on the device, with no server and no sign-in, so it keeps working with no link to Earth. Readings are stored on the device. Every indicator is defined in data, so the screens group them by body system with no extra code. Six kinds of rule (level, trend, sustained, limit, combined and prompt) live in one plain-text file that a clinician can read and edit; the app refuses to load any rule that has no source or no sign-off field. Input checks refuse physically impossible readings. No AI model runs inside the app. The same rules engine is also available as a command-line tool, so other software and AI agents get identical answers.

**How often the rules speak up.** We replayed the rules over all 213 past mission days of our SYNTHETIC data, one day at a time. They caught all three changes we had built in: the low fluid on the day it began, the cabin air in 3 days and the slow sleep slide in 14. They also raised about four results per person per week on data nobody changed, which is too many. We report that as found, and the numbers to tune are with our clinicians.

**What is real and what is not.** The haemoglobin variation and ranges, the cabin carbon dioxide limit, the NASA strength floor and career radiation limit, the crew blood counts on the "Real crew data" screen and the radiation prompt days are real NASA inputs. The four demo astronauts' values, the 213 mission days, the cabin carbon dioxide readings and the radiation dose totals are synthetic, made by a script with a fixed seed, and labelled SYNTHETIC on every screen. The rule numbers (band factor 2, trend window of 7 readings, shift of 1 standard deviation, run of 3, combined minimum of 2) are statistical conventions. [Clinicians: confirm before submission which rules are signed off. Until then:] the rules, the explanation card wording (draft, taken from our challenge brief), the tier wording and every value from our clinical lead's build pack v3.3 are placeholders, marked as such on screen. Not built: voice input on our own screens, sensor connections, a rover profile, rules that change by environment, any calibrated or clinical eye test (for example retinal imaging), and any direct bone measurement. This is a hackathon prototype for assessment and next steps. It does not diagnose and makes no clinical accuracy claim. Sources, evidence grades and limits are listed in docs/EVIDENCE.md.

**Why it matters.** NASA reports that “A total of 43 stone events in 29 crewmembers have been recorded by NASA since 2016”, with one episode reported during spaceflight itself (NASA OCHMO Medical Technical Brief MTB-003, page 6). On Earth, around 7 million people, 27% of Australians, live in rural and remote areas (Australian Institute of Health and Welfare). NASA's Human Research Program names distance from Earth as one of the five hazards of human spaceflight, because medical care becomes harder to receive the further a crew travels. [The team's own story, told by our doctor, with the family's agreement. No child's name, image or voice.] What is different here: most health tools show a number and stop. This one watches the slow trend a single reading hides, treats the wait for Earth as part of the care, and shows how long a reply will take, what to do meanwhile, and what Earth will see. NASA and partner agencies already work on medical decision support for crews; our contribution is a crew-facing sketch of one slice of it. In very remote Australia the same design could help someone hours from a hospital.

**The team.** Four clinicians (digital health, retrieval medicine, telecare, crew physician) and an aerospace engineer.

**Tools and languages.** TypeScript, React, Vite, a rules file in YAML, Python for data preparation and the seeded mission generator, GitHub Pages for hosting. All open source; full list in REFERENCES.md.

## NASA data

What each NASA source decides in the app: OSD-569 sets how wide the haemoglobin range is; NASA-STD-3001 sets when cabin air is Act now, the strength floor and the radiation limit; DONKI sets the days the crew logs a radiation dose; the renal stone evidence report and the urinary health brief are the sources for the kidney stone warning and pathway.

- **NASA Open Science Data Repository (OSDR), OSD-569, Inspiration4 complete blood count (LSDS-7).** https://osdr.nasa.gov/bio/repo/data/studies/OSD-569 . Real blood counts for four crew, three draws before launch and four after return. Bundled in the app. It is used in two ways: the variation in these crew members' own pre-flight haemoglobin draws sets the width of the haemoglobin band, and its laboratory ranges set the haemoglobin limit; and the "Real crew data" screen shows the same band rule running on the real crew.
- **NASA DONKI, solar energetic particle events** (Community Coordinated Modeling Center, NASA Goddard Space Flight Center). https://ccmc.gsfc.nasa.gov/tools/DONKI/ . Real space-weather records, bundled in the app. They fill the space-weather card, and the events that fall inside the mission window are listed in the mission data as radiation prompt days.
- **NASA-STD-3001 Volume 2.** The cabin carbon dioxide limit of 3 mmHg (one-hour average). It is a habitat design requirement, not a clinical alert threshold; using it as a prompt is our choice.
- **NASA-STD-3001 Volume 1, in-mission strength.** Skeletal muscle strength at or above 80% of baseline. We use it as the grip strength floor: 80% of each person's own baseline. It is a programme requirement, not a clinical alert threshold; using it as a prompt is our choice.
- **NASA-STD-3001 Volume 1, career radiation limit.** Career effective dose from spaceflight below 600 mSv. Radiation dose is shown as a share of this limit. Dose from earlier flights is not counted.
- **NASA Human Research Program: 5 Hazards of Human Spaceflight; Risks of Human Spaceflight; Ongoing HRP Research in Space.** https://www.nasa.gov/hrp/hazards/ , https://www.nasa.gov/hrp/risks/ , https://www.nasa.gov/hrp/ongoing-hrp-research/ . The hazards and risks pages are linked as sources on every explanation card, beside its hazard chips.

- **NASA Human Research Program, Evidence Report: Risk of Renal Stone Formation (2017), and the Risk of Renal Stone Formation page.** The source for the early warning: three days of low fluid raise a result that names kidney stone risk. https://humanresearchroadmap.nasa.gov/evidence/reports/Renal.pdf and https://www.nasa.gov/directorates/esdmd/hhp/risk-of-renal-stone-formation/
- **NASA Office of the Chief Health and Medical Officer, Medical Technical Brief MTB-003, Urinary Health, Rev A.** Quoted on the kidney stone pathway for prevention and for ultrasound, and the source given to our clinicians for the steps they will write. https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/ochmo-mtb-003-urinary-health.pdf
- **NASA, Risk of Altered Immune System Responses.** Quoted on the Infections and healing screen. https://www.nasa.gov/reference/risk-of-altered-immune-system-responses/
- **NASA Medical Checklist, JSC-48031 (Generic Rev K, PCN-4, 2008).** The source the emergency module's sixteen checklists are adapted from.

NASA does not endorse this project. No NASA logos are used.
